When the workspace is empty, one person completes the initial setup. PolicyOS creates the first sign-in as the **Root account**.

Root has all application permissions and unrestricted employee and assignment-field visibility. At the beginning, that makes practical sense: someone must establish the workspace and decide who else may enter it.

Root is special. It is not a role that an administrator can assign to someone else, and the Root account cannot be edited or disabled through ordinary user management. Other users can still receive substantial administrative responsibilities, but those responsibilities come through roles.

Broad authority is not the same as exemption from every rule. Root must still sign in, satisfy configured security checks for sensitive operations, provide valid data, and obey domain rules such as non-overlapping policy versions. Root may complete supported policy changes without the ordinary user's independent-approval queue, but still uses the application's preview and execution contracts where the workflow calls for them.

## Rachel exists before Rachel can sign in

The company already has an employee record for Rachel. That record holds facts such as her department, employee type, start date, and manager. Policies use those facts to determine assignments.

An employee record is not a sign-in account.

| Record   | What it represents                               | What creating it does                        |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Employee | A person whose work facts drive policy decisions | Adds facts that policies can evaluate        |
| User     | A person who can sign in to PolicyOS             | Creates application access governed by roles |

Creating Rachel as an employee does not create a password, a session, or permission to use PolicyOS. Likewise, a consultant who operates PolicyOS might have a user account without being one of the employees evaluated by workforce policies.

The two records can be **linked** when the same person is both an employee and a PolicyOS user. That link later lets access rules refer to “this user's own employee record” and to the employee's reporting tree. The link itself grants neither permission nor visibility.

## The first delegation

The company grows, and Morgan joins People Operations. Root wants Morgan to maintain employee facts without taking over every responsibility in the workspace.

Root creates a user account for Morgan and assigns at least one role. Merely entering Morgan's name, email, or company job title does not grant authority. The assigned role does.

That gives us the next design question: how do we turn “Morgan maintains employee information” into a reusable, reviewable definition of access?
