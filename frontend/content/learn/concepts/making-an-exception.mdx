Rachel's vacation policies resolve to 20 days. The company makes an individual arrangement to give her 25 days.

The general rules still express what the company wants for everyone else. This decision is specific to Rachel.

You record a **manual override** for her annual vacation allowance:

> Rachel → Annual vacation allowance → 25 days.

**An override is an explicit employee-specific value that replaces the policy result for its field.**

PolicyOS first resolves the policies, then applies the override. Rachel's final allowance is 25 days, and its source is the override. The underlying 20-day policy result can still be explained.

Her pay schedule and application access are separate fields. This vacation override doesn't change them.

Overrides also work on many-value fields, but their replacement behavior matters. Suppose Rachel's policies supply company email and the design tool. If her application-access override contains only a launch tool, the final set for that field contains only the launch tool. To keep all three through an override, its values must include all three.

An override can also supply a field that currently has no policy result.

When you remove the vacation override, the system uses the policies that apply **at that time**. If they still resolve to 20 days, Rachel returns to 20. If the rules have changed, the new result may be different.

An override doesn't resolve a conflict between policies. Conflicting policy values must still be corrected before resolution can succeed.

For the next lessons, assume Rachel's individual arrangement has ended and her vacation override has been removed. Her policies once again determine her allowance.

Next, we'll see how to change those policies without losing track of their earlier meaning.
