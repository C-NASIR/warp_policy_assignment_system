Rachel works full time in the Design department. You want a policy that gives the design tool to people who meet both of those requirements:

> If an employee works full time AND belongs to Design, give them access to the design tool.

**AND means every condition must be true.** Rachel qualifies because both facts match. A part-time designer meets only one requirement, so this rule doesn't match them.

Now suppose the tool should be available to anyone in Design or Engineering:

> If department is Design OR department is Engineering, give access to the design tool.

**OR means at least one condition must be true.** Meeting either requirement is enough. OR also allows both to be true when the conditions can overlap.

You can combine these ideas:

> Full-time AND (Design OR Engineering).

The parentheses put a box around the department choices. First ask whether the person belongs to either department. Then require that they also work full time.

| Employee facts | Does this combined condition match? |
| --- | --- |
| Full-time, Design | Yes |
| Full-time, Engineering | Yes |
| Part-time, Design | No |
| Full-time, Operations | No |

There is one more useful idea: **NOT reverses a condition**. “NOT in Design” means the statement “in Design” must be false. Rachel would not match it while she works in Design.

NOT describes a logical idea. PolicyOS currently supports AND/OR groups and comparisons; it has no general NOT group in its rule builder.

These connections describe the conditions **inside a policy**. They don't decide which policy wins when several policies apply.

Try reading a combined condition aloud before using it. “Full-time employees in either Design or Engineering” makes the intended meaning easy to check.
