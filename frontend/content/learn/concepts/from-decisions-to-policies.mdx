When you hired Rachel, you made a decision:

> Rachel gets paid every two weeks.

Now you hire another employee. You choose the same pay schedule. Then you hire a third person and make the same choice again.

You notice a pattern. Each time someone joins, you are answering the same question with the same answer.

So you write down a rule for your example company:

> All employees get paid every two weeks.

**A policy is a company decision expressed as a reusable rule.**

Your first decision named Rachel. Your policy describes everyone it covers, including people you haven't hired yet.

When the next employee joins, the rule already tells you the answer.

Look closely at that rule. It contains two parts:

- **Who it applies to:** All employees.
- **What happens:** They get paid every two weeks.

We can express the same idea this way:

> If someone is an employee, they get paid every two weeks.

The “if” part is the **condition**. What follows is the **consequence**.

Here, “consequence” simply means the result of the rule: a pay schedule. It doesn't mean a punishment.

That gives us the basic structure we will use throughout this course:

**Policy = condition + consequence.**

Our first policy covers every employee. But suppose you want a vacation policy that covers only full-time employees.

How would you tell whether Rachel belongs in that group?

In the next lesson, we'll look at the facts you need to decide who a policy applies to.
