Your vacation policy says:

> Full-time employees get 15 vacation days each year.

It describes a rule for a whole category of people. Now ask a question about one person:

> What is Rachel's annual vacation allowance?

We have everything we need to answer:

1. Rachel's employment type is full-time.
2. The policy's condition requires full-time employment.
3. Rachel matches, and the policy supplies an annual vacation allowance of 15 days.

With this one rule, the result is:

> Rachel → Annual vacation allowance → 15 days.

**An assignment is the value selected for a particular employee's assignment field.**

The policy gives us the general rule. The assignment gives us Rachel's specific result.

Another full-time employee can receive the same value through the same policy. Each employee has their own assignment, even though the rule is shared.

Rachel can also have assignments for different decisions:

| Employee | Assignment field | Assigned value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rachel | Annual vacation allowance | 15 days |
| Rachel | Pay schedule | Every two weeks |

These answer two separate questions about her employment. The pay schedule records the arrangement; the actual payment is carried out through payroll.

If Rachel asks why she receives 15 days, you can follow the reasoning back: she works full time, and the vacation policy gives full-time employees that allowance.

You've now connected the first five lessons:

**A company makes a decision, expresses it as a policy, checks employee facts against its condition, and produces an assignment.**

So far, our condition has checked one fact. What if a policy should cover people who work full time **and** belong to the Design department? Next, we'll learn how to combine conditions.
