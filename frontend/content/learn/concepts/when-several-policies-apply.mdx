Rachel can match more than one policy at a time.

Your company already has a pay policy and a vacation policy. You also have a rule for design-tool access. Each answers a different question about Rachel's employment, so all three can contribute to her assignments.

Now the company introduces another vacation rule:

> Employees with at least two years of service get 20 vacation days each year.

Suppose Rachel has now worked at the company for two years. She is still full-time, so she matches both vacation policies:

| Matching policy | Annual vacation allowance it supplies |
| --- | --- |
| Standard Vacation: full-time employees | 15 days |
| Experienced Employee Vacation: at least two years of service | 20 days |

We have answered **which rules cover Rachel**, but we still need to decide her final vacation allowance.

The two matching policies supply **candidate values**. A candidate is a possible result that must be considered before the final assignment is selected.

This gives us two separate steps:

1. **Matching:** Find the policies whose conditions Rachel meets.
2. **Resolution:** Decide which candidate values become her assignments.

A policy can match Rachel without supplying the value ultimately selected for a field. “This policy applies to her” therefore doesn't always mean “this policy determines her final allowance.”

We also consider each assignment field separately. A vacation-policy disagreement is about the vacation allowance. The pay schedule answers a different question.

Before choosing between 15 and 20 days, we need to establish what kind of answer the vacation field accepts. Should it contain one allowance, or can it contain several values?

That is the next lesson.
