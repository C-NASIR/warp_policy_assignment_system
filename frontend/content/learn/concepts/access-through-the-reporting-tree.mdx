Rachel now manages Devon, and Devon later manages Sam. The employee records form a reporting tree:

> Rachel → Devon → Sam

The company wants Rachel to work with her whole organization without seeing an unrelated team.

Three pieces make that possible:

1. Rachel has a PolicyOS user account.
2. That account is linked to Rachel's employee record.
3. An assigned role grants the needed action permissions with **linked employee and reporting tree** scope.

With that scope, Rachel's visible population includes Rachel, her direct report Devon, and her indirect report Sam. A peer manager and that peer's team remain outside the tree.

The reporting relationship supplies the population, but it does not supply authority. Making Rachel Devon's manager on the employee record does not create Rachel's user account, give Rachel employee-reading permission, or assign a manager role. An administrator must configure those separately.

It also helps to keep three different mechanisms apart:

| Mechanism | What it expresses |
| --- | --- |
| Reporting relationship | Who manages whom in the employee hierarchy; it can define access scope |
| Employee group | An explicit collection used to give members another route to attached policies |
| Access role | Which PolicyOS actions and data scopes a user receives |

Putting Devon in the Product Launch group does not make Rachel Devon's manager. Calling a role “Product Launch manager” does not add anyone to that group. Each mechanism has its own job.

Reporting-tree visibility is recalculated from the current hierarchy, so moving an employee to a different manager can change who sees that employee. The same employee edit may also change policy eligibility for other reasons. Access and assignment effects should both be reviewed.
