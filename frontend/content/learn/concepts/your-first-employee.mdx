You run a company. Today, you hire your first employee, Rachel.

You agree on the job and the salary. But there are still everyday questions to answer:

- How often will Rachel get paid?
- How much vacation time will Rachel get?
- Which software will Rachel be able to use?

Someone has to decide these things. For now, that someone is you.

Let's take the first question: **How often will Rachel get paid?**

For this example, you decide:

> Rachel gets paid every two weeks.

That is a company decision. You have taken an unanswered question and chosen an answer.

You make a few more decisions. Rachel gets 15 vacation days each year. Rachel can use the company's email and design software. These are fictional choices for our example company.

Together, these decisions describe part of what working at your company means for Rachel: when money arrives, how much time off is available, and which tools are available to do the job.

With one employee, you can keep track of each decision individually.

Then you hire a second person.

The same questions come up again. Then a third person joins, and you answer them again.

You notice that some answers stay the same. You want everyone to get paid every two weeks.

**Could you make that decision once and reuse it for each new employee?**

That is where the next lesson begins: turning a repeated decision into a policy.
